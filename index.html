Teaching machines to see.

Computer vision turns pixels into understanding: what is in an image, where it is, and what it is doing. Try it on the street scene below.

    In simple words

    Computer vision is the skill of getting a computer to understand photos and video, the way you recognise a friend’s face or a stop sign without thinking about it.

    A computer is not born knowing what a dog is. We show it thousands of pictures labelled “dog” and “not dog”, and it slowly learns which patterns matter, such as ears, fur and shape. This is called training. Afterwards it can recognise dogs in photos it has never seen.

    How a machine sees

    A camera gives a computer a grid of numbers, nothing more. A vision model learns patterns in those numbers from many labelled examples, then applies them to images it has never seen.

    This tiny 8×8 picture is just 64 numbers, from 0 (black) to 255 (white). A colour photo has three numbers per pixel: red, green and blue.

    From those numbers, the model builds up understanding in layers. Here is how a cat might be recognised:

    → →

    1. Capture

      An image is a grid of pixels, each holding red, green and blue values.

    2. Preprocess

      Resize, normalise and sometimes augment the image so training is stable.

    3. Extract features

      Early layers find edges and textures. Deeper layers combine them into parts and objects.

    4. Predict

      The final layers output labels, boxes or masks, each with a confidence score.

    What vision models do

    “Image recognition” covers several different jobs. The demo above shows the first three.

    Classification

    Assigns one label to the whole image, such as “street scene”.

    Object detection

    Finds each object and draws a box around it, with a label and a score.

    Segmentation

    Labels every pixel, so the outline of each object is exact, not a rectangle.

    Pose estimation

    Locates joints and limbs to track how a body moves.

    Optical character recognition

    Reads printed or handwritten text from photos, scans and signs.

    Face and image retrieval

    Compares images by learned similarity to match faces or find near-duplicates.

    Milestones

    1. 1998, LeNet. Yann LeCun’s convolutional network reads handwritten digits on bank cheques.
    2. 2012, AlexNet. A deep network wins the ImageNet challenge by a wide margin, trained on GPUs. Deep learning takes over the field.
    3. 2015, ResNet. Skip connections make networks with over a hundred layers trainable and surpass human-level scores on ImageNet classification.
    4. 2020, Vision Transformer. Transformers, first built for language, are applied to image patches and match convolutional networks at scale.
    5. 2021 onward, multimodal models. Models such as CLIP and the Segment Anything Model learn from images paired with text or prompts, and handle tasks they were not trained for.

    Where it is used

    🩺 Healthcare

    Flagging suspicious regions in X-rays, retinal scans and pathology slides for a clinician to review.

    🚦 Transport

    Driver-assistance systems read lanes, signs and pedestrians. Autonomous vehicles combine cameras with radar and lidar.

    🌾 Agriculture

    Drones and field cameras spot crop disease, count plants and guide targeted spraying.

    🏭 Manufacturing

    Cameras on the line catch scratches, missing parts and misaligned labels at production speed.

    🛒 Retail and logistics

    Barcode-free checkout, shelf monitoring and automatic parcel sorting.

    🦯 Accessibility

    Apps describe scenes aloud and read text for people with low vision.

    Words you will meet

    Pixel
    One tiny dot of an image. A photo is millions of them.
    Model
    The program that has learned patterns from examples and makes the predictions.
    Training
    Showing a model many labelled pictures until it gets good at the task.
    Label
    The name a model gives something, such as “car” or “dog”.
    Bounding box
    The rectangle drawn around a detected object.
    Confidence
    A score from 0 to 1 showing how sure the model is. 0.97 is very sure, 0.31 is a guess.

    Limits to keep in mind

    Models are only as good as their training data. Unusual lighting, rare objects and skewed datasets cause errors, and small, deliberate changes to an image can fool a model. Face recognition and surveillance also raise privacy and fairness concerns, so accuracy should be tested across the people and conditions a system will meet, and a human should stay in the loop for high-stakes decisions.
